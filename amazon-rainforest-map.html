🌳 The Amazon's 3 Major Forest Types

The Amazon isn't one uniform forest — it's split into three types based on whether and how it floods. This is a real, zoomable map: pan/zoom freely, toggle each forest-type overlay, and look near Manaus to see where a whitewater river and a blackwater river physically meet — the closest thing to a real "intersection" of forest types.

Use the layers control (top-right of the map) to toggle each forest type on/off. River-corridor overlays are illustrative floodplain buffers, not surveyed boundaries — real floodplain width varies a lot by season and location.

Terra Firme Forest

Example: Adolpho Ducke Forest Reserve, near Manaus

Upland forest that never floods — roughly 85–90% of the whole Amazon basin. Poor, ancient soils, but the highest tree-species diversity of the three types.

Never floodsPoor soil

Várzea Forest

Example: Mamirauá Reserve, near Tefé

Floods seasonally along whitewater rivers (like the Solimões/Amazon) carrying fertile Andean sediment. Richest soils in the basin.

Seasonal floodFertile silt

Igapó Forest

Example: Anavilhanas Archipelago, Rio Negro

Floods along blackwater rivers (like the Rio Negro) — acidic, sediment-poor, tannin-stained water. Slower growth, specially adapted trees.

Longest floodNutrient-poor

💧 Where they meet: Encontro das Águas

Just outside Manaus, the dark, blackwater Rio Negro (feeding igapó forest) meets the pale, sediment-rich Solimões (feeding várzea forest) and flows side-by-side for kilometers before mixing — with terra firme upland on both banks. Click to fly there and see the overlay corridors overlap.

River courses and floodplain corridors are simplified for illustration (buffered ~6–8 km around approximate river center-lines), not surveyed hydrological data. Reserve boundaries are approximated as circles sized to each reserve's real published area. Forest-type classification follows the flooding-regime scheme first described by botanist Ghillean Prance (1980). Map tiles © OpenStreetMap contributors.
