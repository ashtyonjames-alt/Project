🌳 The Amazon's 3 Major Forest Types

The Amazon rainforest isn't one uniform forest — ecologists split it into three main types based on whether and how it floods. Click a pin or a legend chip to explore each one, with a real example location.

Terra Firme Forest

Example: Adolpho Ducke Forest Reserve, near Manaus, Brazil

"Terra firme" means "firm ground" — this forest sits on higher terrain that never floods. It makes up roughly 85–90% of the whole Amazon basin, making it the dominant forest type by far.

Soils here are old, deeply weathered, and nutrient-poor, so trees rely heavily on rapid nutrient cycling from leaf litter rather than fertile soil. Despite poor soil, terra firme holds the highest tree-species diversity in the Amazon because conditions have been stable for very long stretches of time.

Várzea Forest

Example: Mamirauá Sustainable Development Reserve, Amazonas, Brazil

Várzea forest floods seasonally along "whitewater" rivers like the Amazon/Solimões, which carry nutrient-rich sediment washed down from the Andes. Floodwaters can rise 6–10 meters a year.

Because the sediment is fertile, várzea soils are the richest in the Amazon — historically important for farming and fishing communities, and a key feeding ground for fish that eat fallen fruit during high water.

Igapó Forest

Example: Anavilhanas Archipelago, Rio Negro, Brazil

Igapó forest floods along "blackwater" or "clearwater" rivers such as the Rio Negro — dark, tannin-stained, acidic water carrying very little sediment. Some igapó areas stay flooded most of the year.

Low nutrients mean slower growth and different, specially adapted tree species (many with stilt roots or floating seeds). It's famous for the "meeting of waters" near Manaus, where the dark Rio Negro and pale Solimões flow side-by-side without mixing for kilometers.
